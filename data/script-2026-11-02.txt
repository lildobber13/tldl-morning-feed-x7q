Let's move to robots, because there are three stories there, and they connect nicely.

The first is a peek at where big tech wants to go. Meta reportedly built a humanoid robot prototype meant to handle household chores. It's called Max, and it was built with help from a Chinese robotics company in roughly three months. It runs on Nvidia's Jetson Thor chips, weighs about 106 pounds, and stands about 1.5 meters tall. It has a display that can show facial expressions, a built-in speaker, and hands designed to pick up small objects. Meta is already working on a second prototype. That one gets a redesigned torso and hands, plus a projector and additional sensors to help it handle stairs better. Stairs, of course. Every home robot's nemesis.

The second robot story is about self-driving cars going global. Waymo has picked the Toyota bZ4X as its next robotaxi, starting in Japan in 2027. Toyota will build a custom version of the electric SUV with the Waymo Driver integrated right on the production line.

The third is a research angle that I find fascinating. A piece called Robotics' New Era After Astra says that G P T six Astra can outperform several specialized robotics models. Now to the people who build software. This next one matters because it shows a hidden cost of the AI boom. Postgres 19 is late, and the author says AI is why. It most likely won't ship until November, and it could slip further. Several large features were reverted quite late in the beta. AI has made it easier to find bugs, which means a lot more bug reports for the committers to work through. That pulls their focus away from new features. And the project cares about quality, so quality wins.

A related question is how to hire the next generation of engineers. Here's why it's interesting. If AI can write code, what are you actually testing for in an interview? One post proposes an answer. In product engineering, figuring out the requirements is still most of the job. So the interview has the candidate talk to an LLM that roleplays as a customer, and the candidate has to extract the requirements. The approach rewards people who think of edge cases, come at problems sideways, and consider whole systems at once. It's relatively cheap, and it can sit alongside a standard human interview. For someone who thinks about AI adoption for a living, that's a neat idea to turn over.

Now the dramatic one, and it's a heavy story. It matters because it's a case study in how a crisis plays out in real time. The New York Times has a deep dive on how Binance's 6.3 billion dollar hack went down. It started with a string of unusual transactions on Thursday afternoon that began to cause alarm. Top executives set up an informal war room with about 100 participants and worked through the night. The company had to halt deposits and withdrawals as more than 6 billion dollars drained from the exchange. The article is based on interviews with people who know what happened, plus analyses from crypto forensics firms. It's described as one of the most damaging crises in Binance's history.
