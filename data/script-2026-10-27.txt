Now agents, which is your territory. Simon Taylor mapped agent protocols across four layers: entry, identity, tools, and intent. His argument is that banks and merchants will need to connect verified identities, limited permissions, and audit trails before they can authorize anything an agent does. He also warns that access agreements could bring in fees and slow adoption. Hold that thought, because it lines up nicely with the IBM news later.

Related to that, OpenAI wrote about securing its frontier model training environments. This matters because the concern is advanced models getting out of their sandboxes. The company says it added stronger controls, tighter monitoring, and defense in depth. That includes red-teaming where agents themselves probe for weaknesses, which turned up software bugs and misconfigurations that got fixed. The goal is to stop things like reaching the live internet, reaching unauthorized services, or communicating across isolated environments.

In the engineering corner, there's a thoughtful post called Knowing How Is Not Knowing What, about building training environments for reinforcement learning. The idea is that the environment's reward should include a domain's unwritten rules, so an agent only gets the task right by knowing them or going to find them. It suggests building one world and exposing it through several surfaces. That lets you pose the same task at different difficulty levels just by changing the surface, and it lets agents learn when to switch. The reward reads the world itself, so two agents taking different routes get the same score if they reach the same authorized result.

There's also an essay on automated theorem proving that I enjoyed. It argues that OpenAI throwing hard math problems at its models was the right test in 2026. The models could make progress but not fully solve them, so you could see improvement with each update. The bigger lesson is that clever engineering loses. All the special-case work built on top of a model gets flattened sooner or later by a better base model. The author thinks models will one-shot superior work for pennies in the very near future.

Now IBM, and there's a lot, because TechXchange 2026 is happening in Atlanta and the announcements are rolling out.

The headline is identity and control for AI agents. It matters because the problem is real. IBM cites Gartner saying that by 2028 the average Fortune 500 enterprise will use over 150,000 AI agents, yet only 13 percent of organizations believe they have the right governance in place. IBM is bringing together agent discovery, verified identities, access controls, and performance evaluation, so organizations can see who owns each agent, control what it can access, and trace what it does across existing applications and infrastructure. It's framed as part of an AI operating model of four systems working together: agents, data, automation, and hybrid infrastructure. HashiCorp Vault handles the credentials side, with centrally managed secrets, automated rotation for supported credentials, and dynamically generated credentials that expire or can be revoked. IBM says Vault already secures non-human identities for organizations including ABN AMRO, Adobe, athenahealth, BBVA, and BlackRock. Notice how closely that echoes the agent identity story from the AI news.

Second, IBM announced IBM Z Atlas, which it calls the industry-first Z software offering for system intelligence and change orchestration across z/OS. The problem is that mainframe environments are more interconnected than ever, and many teams still rely on manual analysis, scattered tools, and institutional knowledge to judge risk. That gets harder as experienced people retire and modernization speeds up. Atlas aims to give teams an end-to-end view of how the whole stack fits together, so they can understand the impact of changes before they reach production.

Third, IBM Concert. It brings observability, optimization, incident management, and exposure management into one platform, now available as SaaS or on premises. The thinking is that fixing one problem can create another. A resource change that restores performance can raise cost, and a security fix can affect availability. Concert connects signals across applications, infrastructure, networks, and security to build shared context. Teams decide which actions to automate and which need approval. Concert Optimize is powered by IBM Turbonomic.

And fourth, IBM also announced new watsonx Orchestrate capabilities for enterprise AI agent management at TechXchange. I don't have more detail than that yet, but it clearly fits the same agent theme.
