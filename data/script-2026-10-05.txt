On to the news. I'll start with AI, and a lot of it today comes back to one idea: who controls the models and where they run.

That's why the Aleph Alpha story is worth your attention. Governments and regulated industries want capable models they can run themselves. Aleph Alpha just released Kolibri, an open-weight model for English and German. It's a Mixture-of-Experts model with 78.1 billion parameters, it supports tool calling, and it handles contexts up to one million tokens. It's on Hugging Face under the Apache 2.0 license, and it's aimed at sovereign, mission-critical work in government and regulated industries.

A related piece makes the case that smaller models are the future of AI sovereignty. It's written from an Australian point of view, but the argument travels. Sovereignty means keeping control and choice, not leaning on outside companies for critical capabilities. The author says smaller, specialized models are more adaptable, cheaper, and easier to govern than frontier models. The suggestion is a portfolio of open-weight models, so you get managed interdependence without depending on a single provider.

There's also a story on the infrastructure side. Prime Intellect launched Prime Inference, which matters if you care about open models being served reliably at scale. It covers both serverless endpoints and reserved capacity, running on GPU infrastructure across multiple data centers. The company says the same platform already powers its large-scale reinforcement learning rollouts, synthetic data generation, evaluations, and long-running coding agents. It reportedly processes nearly a trillion tokens internally every day. Their stated mission is to build frontier open models and an open stack for continuously improving agents.

A quick note on keeping big GPU fleets busy. AI21 described how it manages a 10,000-GPU fleet with a queueing tool called Kueue. The company replaced ad hoc negotiations over GPU capacity with queueing and fair scheduling on a shared Google Cloud cluster. A Google Cloud case study says high-priority workloads started 83 percent sooner.

At the small end of the scale, there's Whistle, an open speech recognition model that's just under 17 megabytes. It runs on the same CPU engine as a project called Needle, and it transcribes English, German, French, Spanish, Italian, Dutch, and Polish. At that size it can fit on phones, wearables, robots, smart home devices, cars, and even microcontrollers. There's also Vx, a new systems programming language for heterogeneous computing. It puts hardware topology, memory placement, and reachability into the type system. The idea is to catch bugs at type-checking time that would otherwise show up as a crash, silent corruption, or an out-of-memory error at training step 1,200.

Now the people side. Hiring is a big pressure point for any company adopting AI, and Anthropic is putting money behind it. It's investing $100 million in something called the Claude Frontier Academy, with a goal of training 10,000 AI engineers by 2027. It's partnering with firms like Accenture and Morgan Stanley to build AI fluency and help with enterprise integration. The reason given is rising demand for AI expertise across industries.

And here's a number to put that demand in perspective. A new analysis from Epoch AI asks how many agents could run on all the AI chips shipped through 2027. The answer is tens to hundreds of millions of concurrent frontier-model agents. That could supply as many weekly working hours as roughly 140 million to 720 million full-time employees. Using even a modest share of that capacity would take a massive jump in global demand for AI. If 20 percent of the central estimate were used, it would imply between 2.6 trillion and 5.3 trillion dollars a year in API-equivalent spending.

Next, Meta and its Muse models, which show up across today's news. Meta open-sourced code so developers can build its Muse AI agent into custom gadgets. Meta also says it worked with mathematicians to solve open research problems using its Muse Spark models. I'll come back to Muse in the tech news in a moment.

Two smaller research items. Google described a new Federated Learning system that offers externally verifiable privacy guarantees. It shifts more of the computation to the server, which improves training speed, accuracy, and device coverage. And a paper called UniEvo-VL has a single multimodal model act as both teacher and student, using its own critiques as extra information while it improves itself at test time.

Two more ideas to chew on. As agents do more, services will need to know which agent is acting and whether a real person authorized it. World ID is pitching a way to delegate privacy-preserving proof-of-human credentials to agents, for access, limits, and approvals. And one essay argues that human intelligence is a recent evolutionary accident, while AI can optimize directly against explicit goals and quickly pass us on measurable tasks. The harder problem for people, it says, is deciding which goals are worth optimizing without steering models toward the wrong capabilities.

Now the broader tech news, starting with something that bears on trust in the AI labs. David Robinson resigned from OpenAI this week. He headed the writing of the safety reports the company publishes with each major launch. In a piece in The Atlantic, he says he quit because the culture is broken, and he joins a string of former colleagues who think the company's path is unacceptable. His argument is that OpenAI has thrived by trial and error, but that guarantees periodic failures, and the scale of those failures is growing. Getting things much closer to right the first time matters more because iterating after a mistake may not be possible.

In a similar vein, there's a profile of the people who track AI going wrong. The Wall Street Journal wrote about the swarm chasers, a loose group of online sleuths who piece together connected AI swarm incidents. And Simon Willison argues we'll need default hard budget caps on pretty much everything. People who want to live dangerously should be able to, but only by opting in.

Back to Meta. The Journal profiled Alexandr Wang, who's 29 and was hand-picked by Mark Zuckerberg last year to oversee Meta's AI efforts. He's the company's first Gen Z senior executive, and his internet-flavored style has helped build hype for Muse, partly through an unconventional online campaign. The challenge now is keeping that momentum as rivals push their own products. One analysis says what Meta got right is that none of Muse's capabilities are new. Meta just put the pieces together well.

On the Microsoft side, one essay asks whether this could finally be Microsoft's moment. The argument is that Microsoft could win the enterprise harness with Copilot while the labs compete to supply the intelligence underneath.

At Apple, the leadership picture is shifting. Apple CEO John Ternus is spending a lot of time on hardware and software design. Bloomberg reports he's largely leaving finance to CFO Kevan Parekh and the supply chain to COO Sabih Khan. One of his first priorities was restoring the standing of the design studio, and Apple isn't looking to hire an outside chief design officer.

Over in science, Neuralink shared how it's improving its brain-computer interface decoders. They're machine learning models that turn neural activity into intended action, and each user has to calibrate before using the device. Since clinical trials began, Neuralink has collected over 50,000 hours of unlabeled, freeform neural data. It used that data to pretrain its models, which makes the decoders more accurate and longer-lasting.

Then there's space. The New York Times reports that the US and China are racing to put a nuclear reactor on the Moon, because power is the centerpiece of any lunar base. NASA wants contractors preparing to build a reactor that can survive the trip and run without maintenance near the lunar south pole by December 2030. Both countries see control of the Moon's resources as vital to going deeper into the solar system.

On a skeptical note, a piece in Breakthrough Journal argues that a series of Harvard studies linking nuclear power plants to higher cancer rates is nonsense. The author says the methodology would show increased risk no matter what landmark you used, so it proves nothing about cancer incidence. The lesson is that sophisticated statistics can produce extremely precise nonsense when the design doesn't identify a causal mechanism.

For developers, there are two items. e2e is an open-source AI testing framework for web and mobile apps. It remembers what agents did and replays those actions when it can, without calling the model again, which means fewer tokens and lower costs. You can pick your own model and customize the testing agent. And Nolan Lawson asks why more developers don't "use the platform," meaning built-in web standards. His answer is that browsers spent years playing catch-up, people stick with familiar tools, and libraries often fill a real gap between framework ergonomics and the platform underneath.

Two quick ones to finish. An essay called "So You Think You Could Be An Electrician?" says the trades have physical demands and uncertain rewards that outsiders don't fully understand. And Neko Health, the body scan startup from a Spotify billionaire, has come to America. It uses advanced technology to screen for issues in the blood, skin, and metabolism.

Now the IBM news, and it fits today's theme nicely. On October 1st, IBM announced self-hosted deployment for IBM Bob. This matters for organizations that handle sensitive source code or regulated data and can't send it to public AI platforms. Bob is IBM's agentic software development platform, built to go beyond code generation into software delivery and modernization. With the self-hosted option, companies can run it on premises, in a private cloud, in a sovereign cloud, or in air-gapped environments. That means the code, application context, and data stay where they already live. IBM cited a Futurum Research projection that hybrid and edge deployments will capture 44 percent of the AI infrastructure market by 2030, while public cloud share falls to 46 percent.

Also on October 1st, IBM elected Frank Baker to its board of directors, effective that day. He's 53 and a co-founder and managing partner of Siris Capital Group, a private equity firm that has deployed more than 9 billion dollars of equity capital across technology, telecommunications, and data-focused companies. Before Siris, he was a managing director at Ripplewood Holdings and started his career in mergers and acquisitions at Goldman Sachs. He holds an MBA from Harvard and an economics degree from the University of Chicago. And a local note: he was born and raised near Detroit.
