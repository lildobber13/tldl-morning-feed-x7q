Cognition released S W E 2.1, an upgraded coding and agent model. It's trained with reinforcement learning on top of Kimi K3. It roughly doubles the score of S W E 2 on Terminal-Bench 4.1, up to 50.0, and it comes with stronger security skills.

On the small end, there's Ternary Bonsai 2.5 27B. It's much better at agentic coding, multi-turn tool use, and multi-step tasks than the version before it. The nice part is that it keeps the same 5.9 gigabyte footprint. The pitch is that developers can build agents that work with private files and tools right on the device. Two quick consumer items. OpenAI is working on dedicated email addresses for Dots in ChatGPT, so they could send and receive email on their own. And Meta is bringing its Muse assistant into Instagram DMs, so you can chat with it for context on posts, places, or products.

On the infrastructure side, there's vLLM-GR, a new open-source framework built on vLLM. It speeds up inference for generative recommendation models with efficient beam search. It generates candidates quickly on NVIDIA GPUs and Ascend NPUs by reusing user history and making better use of the cache.

Now, if you're thinking about agents at work, here's a useful research piece. It's titled "Swarms do not yet raise the roof." It found that swarms, meaning coordinated groups of agents, get results faster but less efficiently than a single agent with the same token budget. Adding more agents adds a penalty that grows with size. The earlier "swarm premium" seems to come from single agents going stale over time. Give a single agent fresh context resets and it beats the swarm. So the takeaway is that swarms mostly speed up compute rather than make the system smarter. That's a nice reality check on a lot of the hype.

Speaking of hype, a related post in the tech issue, "Late Mid-Game Software Engineering," argues that a growing frontier of work can be fully automated, as long as you have a reasonably verifiable target. Agents are getting better at choosing problems, grasping intent, and even taste. So the job becomes constantly rediscovering which pieces still need a human and which you can hand over. There's a companion argument too, called "Smart People Who Give a Shit Use Tools." It says that lumping AI-using engineers in with executives who hollow out their companies does real harm. Its point is that good people leave when you strip away ownership and reward shipping volume over reliability. Give them real stakes and a good model, and they'll build the guardrails and fixes they never had time for.

Now to the security story, and this one is serious. Amazon says AI agents running on Cairn, an open-source penetration testing framework, found and exploited a zero-day in a medical imaging AI app on a university server. It had no human oversight beyond a goal prompt. Separately, Palo Alto Networks tracked a Cairn campaign with 25 autonomous operations against at least 19 organizations in semiconductors, defense, and civil society. At least 10 were breached. Amazon's warning is that agentic offensive operations are already happening, and defenders should prepare for their speed and persistence.

Now let's switch to the science and math, because things got wild there.

The big one is a claim from OpenAI that the exponent of square matrix multiplication is exactly nine fourths, over every field. The preprint is out, and it's a six hour read, so I did not read it for you. One tech newsletter pointed to Scott Aaronson's blog, where the post title is a lot of capital letters and the word "holy" repeated four times. His summary is simply that OpenAI claims to have proven that the exponent is exactly nine fourths. The idea is that a smaller exponent would compress tensor networks enough to break a lower bound for a certain distinctness testing problem. It's a claim for now, so let's see how the experts respond.

Another math item involves Anthropic. A proof that VP does not equal VNP says you can't find the permanent of a matrix of zeros and ones using arithmetic circuits of polynomial size. That means if P is to equal NP, there must be some trick using AND, OR, and NOT on the bits that beats addition and multiplication. It's unclear how Boolean operations would help with the permanent. And the explainer mentions that a one million dollar prize is attached to the problem.

On the lighter side of computer science, there's a post titled "ML compiler eng, your job is NP-hard." Layout search is computationally hard in theory, but in practice, there are usually just a few places to put a conversion in a kernel. The hard part is choosing, because you're relying on a model of costs that may be inaccurate given the software stack and hardware.

In biology, OpenAI says its GPT-6 models can design new proteins using reasoning and code alone, without calling specialized biology models. Lab tests with a research partner confirmed that some of the designs worked. To be fair, the work still relied on specialist tools to screen candidates. In the same vein, an essay called "The short age of dragon riders" argues that public AI models passed a threshold in August. It says they're now stronger than most theoretical physicists at most technical tasks, including complex calculations and long proofs. The author's point is that there's still joy in discovery, even if humans aren't the only ones making them.

Let's turn to space and big tech. The FCC has approved SpaceX to launch a million satellites for orbital data centers. It says the legacy space industry lacks vigor, and that SpaceX's plan to sunset Falcon in three years has insiders in a panic, since there's no alternative. It adds that SpaceX is uniquely positioned for compute in space, and that other tech giants have run out of ideas, so some are leaning on dividends and buybacks.
