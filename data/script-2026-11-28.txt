Let's turn to the research. Andrew Trask has a long piece on measuring recursive self-improvement, or RSI. His argument is that neither compute allocation nor human intervention tells you whether a training algorithm actually improved, and neither can spot strong recursion before a more capable model is already trained. He proposes two alternatives. One is sample complexity, which can detect the mechanism without building a stronger AI. The other is counterfactual measurement, which looks at how much capability changes when AI is advised by AI tools.

A related piece from Forethought asks whether scale-dependent algorithms cut against a software-only intelligence explosion. Their answer is yes, they do, but with the way AI companies measure algorithmic progress, the effect will be small. They also want AI companies to publish more data so independent researchers can check.

Then there's a pair on how neural networks represent the world. Stanford's Hazy Research has Plato's Key, built on the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, the idea that networks converge toward a shared representation of reality. Their finding is that similar doesn't mean interchangeable, but a rotation and one global scale, fit in closed form, let one LLM use another's representations without gradient descent. Andrew Trask also has a post saying the hypothesis has been validated. Interesting that those two landed together.

On architecture, there's a long piece on model compression and looped transformers. Looped transformers add computational depth without duplicating the backbone parameters. The open question is how to make the extra computation actually matter. There's also an explainer on looped transformers, thinking twice with the same weights. Nice companion.
