And on the transparency front, OpenAI says it will add invisible text watermarks to eligible ChatGPT and Codex outputs in the EU, to meet AI Act transparency requirements. The method is called textGrain, and it subtly alters word selection so generated text can be detected later. The catch is that editing, translation, and short passages can weaken detection. So it's a watermark that doesn't love being edited. Which is, to be fair, most of what people do with text.

Now, IBM. Today IBM and Red Hat announced that Lightwell has identified and remediated more than 400 previously unknown vulnerabilities in widely used Java libraries. They also announced general availability of Lightwell Clearinghouse, which lets enterprise customers submit specific open source dependencies for priority review and remediation.

The reasoning is that autonomous AI agents are getting capable of chaining several lower-risk weaknesses into a more serious attack. So finding vulnerabilities isn't enough. Companies need practical fixes that work with the versions already running in production, without disrupting operations. Red Hat and IBM say they uncovered, remediated, and backported fixes for those 400 plus bugs in widely deployed, production-grade software. The takeaway is that even mature codebases need ongoing attention as threats evolve. For your day job, that's a handy proof point when clients ask what responsible AI-era security looks like beyond a scanner that just produces a long list.
