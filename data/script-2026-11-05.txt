ChatGPT's search volume has reached about 20 percent of Google's. It added 200 million users in the last two months and now sits at 1.2 billion weekly active users. Voice mode alone has 250 million weekly users, thanks to GPT Live updates that make conversations more natural and two way. OpenAI also plans to merge its Chat and Work modes to simplify things. On the creative side, OpenAI launched Images 2.6 in ChatGPT and Codex. It generates images 50 percent faster than before, and it makes GIFs. You can adjust the speed, edit individual frames, and share them. Two new models, Flare and Sunburst, are available through the API. So yes, the future of artificial intelligence now includes reaction GIFs. I'm not mad about it.

OpenAI also says it's using its models on mechanical and electrical engineering, from design through simulation and production. Engineers use models like GPT 6 Astra for device design, CAD models, and performance simulations. They're working with Mazda on vehicle aerodynamics. Related, there's a post from a developer arguing that the viral claim that Astra can read and write weights to improve Qwen is misleading. Short version, hold the hype.

Over at Apple, Siri AI is now open to all developers through the iOS 27.3 beta. Apps can tap natural language processing and personal context understanding. It's not available in the EU or for users under 13, and more languages are coming. Interesting timing, because Bloomberg reports that Apple quietly lowered daily AI spending caps for its own employees last month. Some engineers went from 300 dollars a day to 200, and more cuts may bring some teams down to 100. Non engineering groups may drop from 100 to 25. The reported reason is that few people were using the full 300 anyway. So Apple is selling AI to everyone else while checking the receipts at home. Fair enough.

That cost theme keeps showing up. A Vals analysis asks how much reasoning effort really matters. The finding is that lowering it cuts cost much more than it hurts quality, and models on low effort can match models on high effort at lower cost, though it depends on the task. That's a practical tip for your work, Zach. Turn the dial down and see if anyone notices. A separate post on token pricing argues it looks sustainable, because competition among providers keeps pushing prices down.

But there's a counterpoint. One analysis says the top 1 percent of AI spenders stalled in the third quarter, and so did Anthropic's dollar growth, while adoption across all other firms is still rising. So the whales paused and everyone else kept swimming.

Capacity is the other side of the coin. One estimate says US AI capacity will grow from 38 gigawatts today to an 81 gigawatt ceiling in 2027. And Stratechery looks at the ballot box. Nearly every relevant local measure opposing data centers won or is winning, with North Dakota the exception. The argument is that the constraint on AI is becoming political, and AI may stay scarce for much longer than people expected. Not exactly what the sales decks say.

Now for the builder's corner, where the Agent Counter really earns its keep. Baseten announced Grid for model routing and governance, plus Sandboxes, which are resumable, VM isolated spaces for agent execution. Its Loops product is now generally available for serverless fine tuning and reinforcement learning. Customers keep ownership of the weights they train. Fireworks AI, at its Forge event, announced ultra fast inference up to 1,500 tokens per second, cheaper background processing, and Nexus for reaching multiple models inside existing tools. It also added features for evaluation, fine tuning deployment, and enterprise controlled infrastructure. Agent Counter is at nineteen, and I'm starting to feel like it's counting me.

Google released a Gemini Notebook API. It's a REST interface for creating and managing notebooks and sources, and it can turn web pages, YouTube videos, plain text, and Markdown into persistent notebooks. It's an early access preview, and only personal Google accounts work for now, not Workspace. Another post covers agent state machines, which enforce permissions, approvals, retries, and completion criteria outside the prompt. Basically, don't just ask the model nicely.

On measurement and research, Prime Intellect published lessons from auditing public evaluations. They fixed issues in benchmarks like AutomationBench and OfficeQA Pro V2, swapped fuzzy prose descriptions for JSON schemas, added new grading, and suggested layered monitoring to stop gaming. Meanwhile Anthropic shared progress on provable inference. Today it's nearly impossible to verify which model produced a given output. Cryptographic proofs, specifically a type called zkSNARKs, could let providers prove it, and could help verify compliance. That's a very long read, so maybe a weekend one.

Linum, a two person lab trying to train generative video models from scratch, trained a text to image model for about 21,000 dollars. It uses a pixel space architecture called Pyramid JiT that cuts tokens aggressively, and the study tests how it performs when trained longer, as a step toward bigger models and video. Two people and a modest budget. Impressive.

Geopolitics next. DeepSeek plans to pretrain its next model, DeepSeek V5, on Huawei's Ascend chips. Those chips have mostly been used for inference, so this is a real test of whether Chinese accelerators can handle full pretraining of a frontier model. Success could reduce dependence on Nvidia and strengthen Huawei's position.

Now the tech issue, and let's go to space. SpaceX launched a commercial page for Starfall, a disk shaped reentry capsule that can carry up to 1,000 kilograms. It supplies 1 kilowatt of power and connects through Starlink, so researchers can pull data while still in orbit instead of waiting for splashdown. It's aimed at biomedical, pharmaceutical, and materials science customers. The timeline depends on Starship, and SpaceX is preparing Flight 15, where it hopes to catch both stages with the tower. Catching both stages. Casual.

On the same theme, the Guardian looks at why pharma firms are heading to orbit for new cancer drugs. Varda Space Industries runs autonomous factories with miniature labs, no astronauts needed. A capsule separates and returns to Earth when processing is done. Varda has three spacecraft in orbit at once, and plans seven missions in 2027 and ten in 2028. Microgravity is the big advantage.

Now IBM. Confluent, now an IBM company, introduced its Streamhouse architecture, a data architecture meant to keep business context current for AI agents, applications, and analytics. Lightning Tables are now generally available on AWS, which completes Confluent's five pillar Data Streaming Platform: Stream, Connect, Process, Govern, and now Query. IBM says Confluent sits at the center of its data strategy, combining real time streaming with IBM's integration, governance, and AI across hybrid environments. The pitch is that data lakes, warehouses, and lakehouses were built for reporting and batch jobs, but agents need current information from systems that update at different times. Otherwise they can act on outdated or conflicting data, and those mistakes can spread into other workflows. Accenture already uses a Streamhouse architecture powered by Confluent to connect data across its global business, which spans more than 120 countries. Agent Counter, final tally, twenty-eight.
