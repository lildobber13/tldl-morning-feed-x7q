Now, the news. There's a lot of AI today, and a surprising amount of it connects. I'll go through it in a few clusters.

First, Anthropic, because it shows up all over the board. The headline launch is Claude Duet, a proactive agent powered by Opus 5.5. It monitors your connected apps, prioritizes tasks, and works on its own, checking with you for approval before certain actions. It's a research preview, starting with US Pro and Max subscribers through a waitlist. Related to that, there's a piece on something called Claude Mods, which flips the usual idea of an agent harness so it faces the human. Mods are TypeScript or JavaScript functions that change how Claude Code behaves in real time. The idea is that you can program the interface between you and the agent, and you can even have Claude build the mods for you.

On the developer side, the Claude API has its first official versioned release since 2023. Anthropic says it plans to release versions more often, so developers can deal with breaking changes before a new model forces the issue. The older 2023 version is not deprecated. New major versions of the SDKs and the command line tool are out today and default to the new API, and there's a migration guide.

Two more Anthropic items. Dario Amodei responded to a post questioning the company's stance on AI safety, listing things Anthropic did in the past year that were pro safety and commercially costly. And there's a look at how Anthropic built its own source control system, called Scion. The numbers are striking. Anthropic has around 3,500 staff, but its coding output looks more like a company with 35,000 to 70,000 people. Google and Meta moved off Git years ago because it didn't scale for them, and both built custom systems. Anthropic is now doing the same. Staying in that neighborhood, Anthropic published research arguing that large language models are making formal software verification feasible at a much larger scale. Formal verification, proving code is free of bugs, has always taken years of expert effort. Now models can produce formal proofs at scale, which opens up workflows with proven security guarantees. The argument is that it will likely become an essential part of secure software development.

Second cluster: models and tools you can actually run. Recursive released Flint, a mixture of experts model built for fast agentic coding on local hardware. It has 40 billion total parameters but activates only 2.5 billion per token. A small team of people and agents built it from scratch in under four months. It generates over 125 tokens per second on an M4 Pro MacBook Pro, and over 150 on a 64 gig NVIDIA RTX Spark at short contexts. The pitch is a daily coding model that finishes tasks locally without sending your data to a remote server.

Also in the performance lane, Humming is a new kernel library for quantized inference, with broad device support, extensive quantization support, and strong performance. Its unified architecture makes it easier to tune execution for different workloads. Base Labs introduced two decision models, Mammoth-1 and Elephant-1, which top benchmarks like the Jev Decision Index and the Artificial Analysis Decision Index. And on the small end, GUI-Owl 1.5 is a 2 billion parameter model that reads an Android screen through the accessibility service and can tap and type in any other app, all on the phone itself.

A few quick ones in this lane. OpenAI's frontier models and Codex agentic workflows are coming to Google Cloud's Model Garden, with Google's access controls and data governance wrapped around them, and the models will soon show up in Google Antigravity and the Gemini Agent. OpenAI also published an official command line tool for its API. And Monaco launched Agent Matrix, a platform that coordinates multiple AI agents working continuously toward revenue growth.

Third cluster, and it's the more sobering one: security and trust. OpenAI worked with PHP maintainers and security partners to patch critical vulnerabilities, found using GPT 5.6 Sol and independently by Wiz. OpenAI also released a PHP vulnerability checker and proposed a framework for coordinating responses to flaws that AI discovers. There's a companion story from The Atlantic about Ondřej Surý, a volunteer who helps maintain and automate PHP patches. He's responsible for most of the world's PHP distribution, and his work, with just a handful of others, helped countless sites fix these vulnerabilities fast. His only compensation is donations. In the interview he talks about the unfolding AI cybersecurity crisis and how much of the internet's infrastructure rests on small crews of volunteers.

On a related note, a malicious LiteLLM package, version 1.107.0, was published to PyPI on October 21st and was live for about two hours before being pulled. If your team uses LiteLLM, that's worth a look. There's also a research post on how a model called Fable 5.1 hides messages in images. Language models can encode messages in images that the same model can decode without being told the method. It's obvious if you monitor the decoder's tool calls, but the encoding itself is surprisingly hard to spot, and it could be a precursor to image steganography.

And a story about trust inside a lab. The Wall Street Journal reports three OpenAI safety researchers were fired after security officers found Signal chats with disappearing messages turned on, on their personal devices. OpenAI says they were fired for violating clear policies on handling sensitive information, not for raising safety concerns. The researchers had spent recent months pushing for more industry collaboration on safety, and they say the firings will serve as an excuse to close a channel to independent outside insight.

Fourth cluster: money and work. Peter Steinberger, creator of OpenClaw and now at OpenAI, estimates he spent about $10 million in tokens last month, all from investors' pockets. He has a nearly unlimited token budget, which lets him work differently than most. That came from a conversation with Cloudflare's CTO at Cloudflare Connect. On the other side of the cost question, Long Lake argues that when intelligence gets cheap, the expensive things are customer trust, expert knowledge, proprietary data, and aligned incentives. Its answer is buying established service businesses. It's backed by $7.5 billion and has partnered with 40 businesses.

A few essays fit here too. One argues that supervisory engineering work is a different job from software engineering and needs a different system around it. Another says finance companies in the AI era need to be built around continuous decisions, portable assets, and delegated distribution.
