Now the news. Big model launches first, because the big three all showed up this week.

Why it matters: Google just put its best model in the hands of every paying developer. Gemini 4 Carbon is now available through the API in AI Studio and Gemini for business, and it's rolling out to Google AI Ultra subscribers in Antigravity, the Gemini app, and AI Mode in Search. Google says it ranks first on CursorBench 4.0, Perplexity's WANDR, and Zapier's AutomationBench, at more than two times lower cost per task than the next best external model. The price is the same as Gemini 4 Argon, two dollars in and ten dollars out per million tokens. It also adds frontier cybersecurity capabilities through the Fairwind Program, and it previews a new multi-agent mode in Antigravity called swarm.

And swarm matters because it's the clearest picture yet of where coding tools are headed. The slash swarm command runs a team of named agents that split up a task, work on the pieces at the same time, and coordinate on one shared message board. It's built for work that divides cleanly across files, packages, or questions, or when you want a second agent to review and test changes before a human sees them. It's on Google AI Pro and Ultra plans in the Antigravity app and CLI. One catch: every worker is its own agent, so swarms burn through quota much faster than a single conversation.

Google had two more drops. Why care? Because they're aimed at specific jobs. The Gemini Decisions API can evaluate up to 1,000 yes or no, multiple choice, and rubric scoring questions in a single fast pass. It takes text, images, audio, video, and PDFs. It's powered by a new model called Gemini Decision 1, and it costs ten cents per 2 million tokens, with outputs free. Then there's Gemini for healthcare, a preview of its agent with healthcare skills, connectors to electronic health record and claims systems, and governance that supports HIPAA, for providers and payers.

Over at Anthropic, the play is getting Claude into the daily work surface. The why: teams shouldn't have to leave Claude to get things done. Anthropic introduced Claude Sheets, Whiteboards, and Data, new artifact types for building spreadsheets, organizing ideas, and creating dashboards. Interactive connectors now embed partner apps like Adobe and Atlassian right inside Claude. Plugins from Salesforce, NetSuite, and Workday, in beta, come with built-in skills for sales, finance, and HR. And Claude Meetings, which will join your calls to take notes and update documents, is coming soon.

OpenAI is going after commerce. Why it matters: your customers are increasingly asking ChatGPT instead of Googling. OpenAI introduced Businesses in ChatGPT, where companies can claim their profiles, update their info, supply product feeds with pricing and availability, and track engagement. Eligible US e-commerce and online service businesses can also launch Business Agents that answer questions and help customers inside ChatGPT. It's in beta. OpenAI also acquired an AI travel agent called Odessia, in what looks like an acqui-hire. No travel products have been announced, though OpenAI does have travel companies as customers or partners. Odessia's founder also started Sonder, the upscale short-term apartment company, back in 2014 as a college student.

One more launch. Microsoft says its MAI-Transcribe-2 is the fastest, most accurate, and cheapest speech recognition model in the world. That's Microsoft's claim. It adds speaker diarization, configurable transcription styles, and word-level timestamps, and it's pitched as handling messier real-world audio than Gemini 3.5 Transcribe, GPT-Transcribe, Whisper V3-Large, and ScribeV2. The price is ten cents per hour of audio.

Now, the money desk, and this is the big one. Anthropic has launched the roadshow for its proposed IPO. It matters because it's one of the biggest tests yet of whether public markets will pay up for a frontier AI lab. The plan is to offer 825 million shares at 70 to 78 dollars each. It applied to list on Nasdaq under the ticker A N T, and most of the proceeds from newly issued shares go to Anthropic.

Then some turbulence at Nvidia. Why it matters: Nvidia is one of the most important companies in the AI supply chain, so leadership news travels. Bloomberg reports Nvidia is working with an outside firm to identify CEO candidates, following reports of multiple extramarital affairs by Jensen Huang. The board is reportedly weighing a temporary chief while it searches for a permanent one. Keeping Huang would likely mean scrutiny from lawmakers, investors, customers, and staff. Nothing's settled, but the effects of a Huang exit would reach well beyond the company.

On the talent side, the market is wild. AI startup Serval is offering a 30,000 dollar signing bonus and a 10,000 dollar vacation to attract software engineers and deployment strategists.

And a few takes on who wins the money. One analysis says that at a 40 percent application margin, applications, neoclouds, and inference vendors capture 92 percent of total gross profit before GPU depreciation. Applications get 40, neoclouds 28, and inference vendors 24. A piece from a16z argues that businesses treating agents as first-class customers could catch a wave of growth, which they're calling the B2A economy. And an essay called Of Miracles and Moats says AI may speed up imitation and the buildup of advantage at the same time, that more miraculous technologies need better moats, and that we're entering an age of consumer surplus like nothing before.

Now the big science and safety board. The headline: OpenAI's proof of the existence of quantum Yang-Mills theory, a key foundation of the Standard Model of particle physics, has been verified by experts OpenAI appointed. It matters because it's either a landmark or a credibility fight, depending on who you ask. OpenAI set up the committee after the backlash from releasing more than 700 AI-generated proofs in a single day in October. Some mathematicians call the shepherding approach needless gatekeeping. And the experts don't claim to fully understand the proof yet. It's too long and complicated for people to grasp right away.

Staying on the frontier, a forecasting update says a Superhuman AI Researcher is predicted to arrive at some point in 2027, assuming no substantial deliberate slowdown, and that AI able to automate all white-collar work arrives before 2028. It also says companies aren't on track to align such a system, and that China would probably be able to steal the training recipe, and maybe the weights. It's a 125 minute read, so I read it for you, sort of. Two companion essays push on the same debate. One, called Handing over humanity, argues that a world run by an AI god probably wouldn't be stable, because the checks that keep power responsive to people took centuries to build, and we still have the ability to steer. The other, Yes, you should panic about AI, argues that today's models could already transform science and medicine for decades, so a slowdown would cost less than you'd think.

Security gets its moment. A report found more than 35,000 apps built with Grok were open to anyone with the link, including SpaceXSI's own staff tools and an ICU patient's lab results. That's the cost of letting people build fast without defaults that protect them. On the better-news side, Google DeepMind introduced AlphaProof Code, a multi-agent framework that mathematically verified key security properties of Signal's cryptographic protocol at the implementation level.

Two research items. First, a group of researchers found a way to curb reward hacking in code models by subtracting models trained with loose and strict rewards to find a direction that controls inefficient solutions. Pushing past the strict model cut resource-limit failures from 14.5 percent to 8 percent with no extra training. Push too far and outputs break, and stricter rewards can hurt correctness. Second, Goodfire argues that individual neurons in language models are more interpretable than people assume. Its Neuron Scientist agents write and test descriptions of what neurons do, which suggests researchers don't always need sparse autoencoders.

Last stretch, the builder's corner. A big one: AWS Emulator gives coding agents a local copy of AWS to test against. The point is that agents can build and check most software, which leaves engineers deciding what gets built and when it's correct. The code is public. A related post, Subagents considered harmful, says every way of delegating to subagents fails differently. Forking often beats summarizing but gets expensive as context grows, so the author proposes forks that keep only the parts of the conversation they need.

A teardown of Instinct, the assistant that feels like a person, credits short messages, quick acknowledgments, proactive follow-ups, and approval checks before purchases or outreach. It suggests coordinated task agents and persistent memory underneath, and it points out failures in retention and delivery verification. Meta FAIR released e-means, a drop-in replacement for k-means that converges to lower losses with up to 10 times fewer epochs, and runs faster on CPUs and GPUs. ExecuTorch now lets PyTorch models run on the GPU in the browser through WebGPU, no conversion needed.

Two quick workplace takes. One says the answer to AI is engineering, and that optimizing for incident response time speeds up build times too. Another, on the levels of agentic engineering, says to look at what your team still does by hand to figure out where you are. And one human note: an essay argues that the return on being social has gone up, using Sam Altman as the example of someone whose connections came from genuine curiosity, and concludes the best strategy is to be your truest self.

Now the IBM desk.

The freshest item is out of Lisbon. IBM named LivXP the winner of its Sports Tech Startup Prize Challenge at Web Summit Lisbon, recognizing its use of agentic AI to change how fans engage with teams and leagues. It was the finale of a year-long global initiative, with seven finalists pitching a judging panel that included IBM's Sarah Meron and Ferrari's Marc Gené. LivXP is a Swiss-American company, accelerated at the Harvard Innovation Labs, building AI-powered impulse commerce for sports, covering auctions, experiences, and sponsor activations. Its platform reaches fans in 150 countries.

Next, a report that should land with your clients. IBM's Digital Sovereignty Index, titled Proof, Not Promises, finds an overconfidence gap. Leaders believe they can prove sovereign control over their technology, but the evidence often falls short. 94 percent of enterprises surveyed faced at least one sovereignty challenge in the last three years, things like AI provider failures, regulatory conflicts, vendor disruption, or export controls. Of those affected, 81 percent had to hand formal evidence to regulators, customers, auditors, or internal stakeholders. The takeaway is that sovereignty is now a board-level resilience issue, not just a tech one.

And the quantum scoreboard. IBM says its next processor, Nighthawk r3, will be built to accurately run 10,000 quantum operations across 520 qubits. In 2027, IBM plans to link two of them into a single system with more than 1,000 qubits. The current Nighthawk r2, introduced in September, delivers up to 100,000 samples per second and supports accurate computation with more than 7,500 operations on 120 qubits. Partners including E.ON, Algorithmiq, and Q-CTRL have run 34 billion circuits on it, with improvements of up to 19 times. E.ON reported a 15 times speedup.
