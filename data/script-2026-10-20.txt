Now, the news. Let's start with the AI world, where Anthropic had a busy few days of self-reflection.

Anthropic announced plans to put an initial 5 percent of its equity into a new Public Benefit Fund. More contributions would follow as the company's valuation grows. The fund is meant to support things like addressing AI's economic and labor impacts. That's a notable amount of equity to commit, and the labor angle is interesting given the next story.

Anthropic also published research on how AI is reshaping its own workforce. Entry-level workers have fallen from 27 percent to 9 percent of its technical hires since early 2025. Employees say Claude makes them more productive, but all that extra output has shifted their work toward supervising agents. Human attention is becoming the bottleneck. So the machines write the code faster, and now the humans are stuck reading it. Congratulations to everyone involved.

That ties neatly into two pieces on coding agents. One argues that trusting them comes from better code architecture, strong invariants, and automated verification, not from longer prompts or more human review. The idea is to study past agent mistakes and redesign your systems so those mistakes can't happen by default. And the Claude Code team described how it handles code review at that volume. They automated most of the stages, with continuous review, automated verifications, multiple layers of checks before a human gets involved, and a tiered risk system for approvals. Same theme, really. People can't read everything, so build the system so they don't have to.

One more Anthropic item, and it's a fun one. Claude dug into the origins of the ECDSA curve seeds. They turned out to be generated with a C library's lrand48 random number generator, seeded with Unix timestamps. Not hash algorithms, and not a backdoor. So the great cryptographic mystery was, in the end, a mundane random number function. The truth is often less exciting than the conspiracy.

Now to DeepSeek, which had a full week. First, it released Search and Fetch APIs. They give agents and retrieval apps access to live web content. Search finds pages for a query and returns structured results, while Fetch grabs a single page and hands back its content as Markdown. Both are raw JSON APIs, using the same bearer authentication as the main DeepSeek API. Then there's a new model, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017. It improves across reasoning, coding, agentic tasks, and visual benchmarks, and it cuts task execution time and output length. The headline detail is memory. Through some clever cache reuse and a smaller number format, its global KV cache shrinks to 890 bytes per token, and its persistent cache is roughly one eighth of the previous Flash model's. Cheaper to run and better, which is the combination everyone wants.

On the science side, there's a big one. OpenAI's recently released proof of the quasi-Riemann hypothesis is being called the most significant AI-for-math achievement so far, by quite a margin. That's a weaker version of the Riemann hypothesis, arguably the biggest open question in math, and one that would help mathematicians understand prime numbers. The article notes that the vast majority of the consequences of resolving the full thing already follow from solving the quasi version. Not the whole mountain, but a very large chunk of it.

And then there's the story I had the most fun with. A Seattle startup called Endurance Energy demonstrated the feasibility of using an active underwater volcano to generate electricity. In September, it put a 3-ton prototype power plant at the Axial Seamount in the northeast Pacific and generated less than 10 seconds of net-positive electricity. Ten seconds. But hey, it's a proof point. The next trial is planned for winter 2026 to 2027, aiming for more sustained power. The company is also developing a 2-megawatt commercial generator for its first planned deployment, with the Kingdom of Tonga in 2027.

On the engineering side, Cloudflare had two posts. One introduces Clairic, which it calls a new class of extraction models, a System One style of model. They pull out, compute, estimate, and forecast values from unstructured inputs, with higher accuracy, lower latency, and lower cost than traditional LLMs. The answer comes directly from the input, is computed from it, or is predicted beyond it. It can't produce a mistyped answer or hallucinate the way an LLM does, though it can still be wrong. The other post is about cutting the inference bill by 68 percent. Cloudflare dropped its average review cost from $2.43 to 77 cents. The biggest savings came from checking what actually reached the provider on each session's first request, and then ordering those messages as consistently as possible so the cache could actually be used. The lesson is to check whether your first request really reads from the cache, and whether reviewers still catch real problems at the effort level you're paying for.
