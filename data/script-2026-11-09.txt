Now a cluster of pieces about what A I is doing to the work of software, which I know is close to your day job, Zach.

First, a post arguing that large language models are terrible at designing A I systems. In other words, they don't know how to design software that itself uses L L Ms. The author floats a few reasons. Most training code predates L L Ms, or the training environments just aren't good for writing code that calls L L Ms, or current agents simply aren't good enough at software design yet. The takeaway is that L L Ms seem good and bad almost at random, but if you can predict ahead of time where they'll shine and where they'll stumble, you can get enormous value from them. That feels like a pretty practical lesson for anyone helping teams adopt this stuff.

Related, there's a post called Programming and Fatigue. The claim is that programming is much more tiring with A I, and that this is actually a good sign. A I takes the repetitive, obvious parts, so what's left for the developer is the high-judgment work. Of course that's more tiring. That's a nice reframe if you've ever finished a day of prompting feeling oddly wiped out.

Speaking of which, one of the quick links is a post called How I'm Fighting the A I Brain Fry. The author says the usual way of working with A I has sequencing and context problems, and suggests organizing the work like a priority queue instead. It's a short read, about three minutes.

Then there's a longer piece, The Jevons Paradox Has a Second Half. The argument is that the amount of software being created will rise much faster than the number of people paid to make it. A I will enable far more software of a kind nobody used to make. The developer jobs that grow will be the ones the machines made faster. So demand for software can be bottomless, and the work can still move somewhere else. Interesting, and a little uneasy. It's a good reminder that more demand doesn't automatically mean more of the same jobs.

And the next piece cuts the other way. The Wall Street Journal asks whether A I is taking white-collar jobs, and the latest numbers are a surprise. Professional and business services in the U S added 70,000 jobs in October, the largest monthly increase since July 2022. One month isn't a trend, but it's a data point against the doom narrative. I'll take it.

There's another big-picture essay called A I Is Causing Signal Collapse Everywhere All at Once. The idea is that A I makes it harder to tell good work from bad work, and from outright fakes. People lean on signals as shortcuts to make decisions all the time, and A I undermines many of them while lowering the incentive to produce good work. The author says the speed and scale of the collapse is unprecedented, and that losing so many dependable signals will cause large shifts in society. That's a long one, about 20 minutes, but it's a thought-provoking idea. How do you know something is good when everything looks polished?

Now to the IBM news, and there's a nice run of quantum this week.

On Friday, IBM, the University of Chicago, the State of Illinois, and the Illinois Quantum and Microelectronics Park unveiled an IBM Quantum System Two in Chicago. It's IBM's first quantum computer in Illinois and the most advanced system the company has available. It's hosted at first at Hyde Park Labs by the University of Chicago, and it runs on IBM Quantum Nighthawk, the most powerful quantum chip IBM has built. It's the anchor system for the National Quantum Algorithm Center, which Governor JB Pritzker established in 2024 with IBM as a leading member. Eventually it will move to the quantum park campus, which is still being built. Arvind Krishna said it can deliver scientifically useful results to organizations across Illinois and beyond, and called it a milestone for the state's quantum community. Organizations in Illinois are already working with IBM on computationally difficult problems.

Also this past Thursday, IBM, the Quantum Science Center at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, and AMD launched a co-design platform for quantum error correction. It brings quantum and high-performance computing together. The first deployment puts IBM's quantum computers, including the 120-qubit Nighthawk, in the same place as Oak Ridge's Umbra supercomputer, which runs on AMD Instinct M I 355 X G P Us and fifth-generation AMD EPYC processors. The challenge now is scaling up error correction with more classical computing, and this platform lets researchers study how tightly the two kinds of computing can work together. It ties into the Department of Energy's Quantum Genesis Mission, which targets 100 logical qubits through its Q Competition.
