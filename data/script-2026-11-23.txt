Next, a story that sounds like science fiction and is actually a clinical trial. Neuralink has increased the rate of its surgeries and extended its trials to Canada and the U K. The first participants now video chat with new ones to coach them through adjusting to the technology. Here's the catch. The terms of those trials don't include upgrades. Unless Neuralink comes up with a commercial device, participants would have to return to their lives from before the implant. That's a strange kind of beta test, one where you can't keep the product.

Related to the science side, there's an essay arguing that AI in biology is not just a data problem. How far AI gets will depend on how much data we have, but also on what gets measured and how good it is. A pile of systematically incomplete measurements won't give you a full understanding of something as complex as a cell. The author thinks AI will be a powerful tool for studying biology, but there's still no clear path to a complete account of how a whole cell or tissue works. So, more data helps. It just isn't magic.

And a counterweight to all the hype: a writer who doesn't expect superintelligence in 2027. He says the industry leans toward very short timelines, and he figures we're about 60 percent of the way there compared with where we were in 2022. His guess is 2029 instead. Two years sounds like a rounding error, but he argues it matters, because it changes what the industry should prioritize. He thinks pushing aside long-timeline work is a mistake. It's refreshing to hear a forecast that doesn't come with a countdown clock.
