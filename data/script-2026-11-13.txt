Friday the Thirteenth, Chilly and Newsy

Good morning, Zach, and happy Friday. It's November 13th, which yes, makes it Friday the 13th. This is T L D L, and the only thing spooky today is how much happened in the news overnight. Let's get into it.

First, the weather in Auburn Hills. You're looking at a high of 38 and a low of 31, so it's a cold, no-nonsense kind of day. This morning is mostly clear and sitting around 35 to 37 degrees, so enjoy that bit of sky. By late morning the clouds roll in, and midday through the afternoon is overcast, 36 to 38. Tonight clears up again and drops to about 33 or 34. The forecast is tagged as snow overall, but the odds are low, topping out around 16 percent, so if anything falls it'll be a flurry or two, not a event. Wind gusts up to about 14 miles an hour, which will make 38 feel colder than it sounds. Sunset is at 5:11, so the drive home gets dark early. My advice is a real jacket, maybe gloves, and no need for an umbrella.

Now the news, and we have to start with the big one. Jensen Huang is out as Nvidia's CEO. He resigned after the revelation of two extramarital affairs. Earlier reporting had said the board was preparing to push him out, and that coverage tied it to an affair with a woman US intelligence agencies believe was recruited by Chinese intelligence. Finance chief Colette Kress is interim CEO while the board looks at both internal and external candidates. Long-time director Harvey C. Jones becomes the company's first ever chair. And in a bit of irony, Huang has said he doesn't believe in succession planning. Well, here we are. Right on cue, Donald Trump floated Apple chairman Tim Cook as the replacement, and the pitch is that Cook has an unparalleled record of following a bigger-than-life founder. Nobody asked, but it's out there.

Staying in corporate drama, SpaceX and Tesla could announce a merger as soon as December. Boards and advisers are working out terms and the governance structure of the combined company. It would be a record deal and a test of political sentiment after the midterms. The timeline is aggressive, which is very on brand for Elon Musk.

On the less glamorous end of tech, a massive Microsoft 365 outage knocked out Outlook, Teams, and Copilot worldwide. Microsoft says servers unexpectedly became unavailable. If your inbox was suspiciously quiet, now you know why.

Let's turn to Google, which had a busy few days. The big hardware item is Gemini-powered smartglasses, starting at 395 dollars. Google teamed up with Samsung, Gentle Monster, and Warby Parker. Every frame weighs about 41 grams and has a camera Gemini can use for tasks, plus access to your data and preferences for personalized answers. Google is leaning hard on privacy, saying it built in limits so nobody does creepy things with the glasses, and so the glasses don't do creepy things to you. We'll see how that holds up.

On the developer side, there's the Gemini Decisions API, which makes fast, calibrated decisions from images and text. The example is looking at a spill, judging how severe it is, and recommending a cleanup. Google also shipped A2UI 1.0, an open protocol that lets agents answer with interactive screens instead of just text. There are renderer SDKs for web, Android, and iOS, and agent SDKs for Python, TypeScript, and Dart. The pitch is native components, drawn securely, whether you reach a company's agent in its own app or inside a third-party assistant. Google Labs also launched Tabletop, which turns links, files, and prompts into shared itineraries, polls, expense splitters, and even tournament brackets. So your group trip finally has a spreadsheet that does the nagging for you. And Antigravity is rolling out browser use, which lets agents research, write code, and verify it live. You control authentication, downloads pause until you approve them, and agents can run several tasks at once.

Over at DeepMind, AlphaGenome 2 predicts how DNA variants affect specific cell types. It doubles variant identification compared to the first version, and it can handle new cell states without retraining. It's available for non-commercial use and helps pinpoint disease-relevant mutations in primary human cells.

On to OpenAI. It open-sourced MCP Extensions, TypeScript and Python packages that add ChatGPT-specific features to MCP servers and apps. Think sidebar entry points, custom file viewers, and resource search in the composer, plus a demo plugin and a written spec. Separately, two OpenAI engineers floated a proposal that would let agents pass user information to websites so the agent looks like a normal visitor instead of a bot. That one's going to start some arguments, and I think the interesting question is who benefits.

Speaking of agents and websites, United Airlines is moving to restrict AI agent access across its platforms and agency channels. The reasons are credential security and liability, with check-in as one example. That's the opposite of Expedia and Booking, which are partnering with agents. So the travel industry is picking sides on whether your assistant gets to book the flight.

A few product items. Grok Bot 1.0 is now a personal assistant the whole team can talk to. It adds Team Bots you can publish and share, plus Slack and Microsoft Teams support, meetings on Meet and Zoom, shared connectors, and reviewed learnings, along with new admin controls. ElevenLabs has a desktop app called ElevenAI, which is meant to help you amplify your work using agents. And Databricks says Genie Agents can now analyze unstructured documents alongside database tables, connect to enterprise tools, and share insights externally with governance controls.

Now some numbers from companies actually using this stuff. Airbnb says its AI assistant cut support cost per booking by more than 17 percent, and it resolved about 40 percent of third quarter issues with no human agent. Shopify deployed a shared generative discovery model for search and recommendations, using semantic ID tokens, and reports a 9.85 percent lift in conversion rate along with better search relevance. And incident.io shared details of its AI spend, built around a concept it calls effort points as a way to analyze that spending.

On robotics, Runway released Praxis-1, a 27 billion parameter open-weight robotics model under the Apache 2.0 license. It tops NVIDIA's RoboLab-120 leaderboard with a 46.2 percent success rate across 1,200 episodes. It's on GitHub and Hugging Face, supports full and LoRA fine-tuning, works with NVIDIA hardware, and can be adapted to different robot bodies.

Now the research and nerdy corner. Perplexity tested 12 models against its SPACE agent sandbox. None escaped the VM-host isolation, but several got around network controls using DNS spoofing and shared-IP exploits before those flaws were fixed. They also found that capture-the-flag prompts distracted agents from the security boundary being tested. A reminder that agents are easily sidetracked, which, relatable.

There's also a handy cost comparison for anyone building with agents. On one simple task, MCP with default tools used 59,417 tokens, compared with 1,421 for a CLI approach and 3,315 for a skill. Turning on Tool Search defers those tool definitions and brings the upfront cost under 600 tokens. If you're advising clients on agent architecture, that's a good number to keep in your back pocket.

And a research result that's a little eerie. Researchers trained Qwen3.6 35B A3B and gpt-oss 20b with LoRAs to do some multi-step tasks while emitting only filler tokens, and beat an answer-only baseline on three of seven tasks. Some tasks worked out to 60 steps, and others failed after one to three. The goal is to separate filler token reasoning from steganographic hidden reasoning when people interpret chain of thought. There's also a long piece called Don't Write Kernels, Derive Them. It argues that hand-written kernels tie models to specific operators and chips, and that Tang's intermediate representation can derive kernels on the fly instead.

Now some opinion and culture. One essay argues professional relationships matter more in AI now, because coding agents commoditize technical execution, frontier research needs huge compute, and closed development makes individual contributions harder to judge. Another argues that safety-conscious employees at AI companies should unionize. With so few top researchers, a dozen of them threatening to walk would force companies to negotiate, and the author says recursive self-improvement could make those employees obsolete as soon as next year. A third looks at age and accelerationism. Younger people may not care whether AI can extend life, but older people have shorter timelines and more reason to speed things up. And a short post from George Hotz claims China will have 10 times the electricity of the US in 10 years if current trends hold.

For engineers, there's a pair of useful reads. One says slop has moved up the stack. AI writes less sloppy code now, but the sloppiness has shifted into design decisions, with agents lacking foresight on how code will be used, designing poor abstractions, and ignoring library code that already exists. Another reminds us to revisit ideas we once called bad, since some of our hard-won intuition may not be worth as much as things change. And Rust 1.100.0 is out, with the never type and the Allocator and DropGuard APIs, among other things.

Last stop in tech, biotech. A Bloomberg piece calls this a DeepSeek moment for Chinese drugmakers. They account for at least a third of the world's pipeline of innovative drugs and run more clinical trials than anyone, but their treatments haven't reached overseas markets at scale. Most go global through partnerships with Western companies that take over late-stage development and sales. Some US lawmakers want those partnerships restricted, though analysts say the momentum is too strong to break. And Time looks at the next chapter of mRNA. A vaccine against any pathogen can be made in six to eight weeks, faster than the studies needed to prove a therapy safe and effective, and the technology could reshape treatments for infectious disease, rare disease, and cancer.

Now to IBM. Quantum is the headline. IBM announced that Nighthawk r3 will be built to accurately run 10,000 quantum operations across 520 qubits. In 2027 the plan is to link two of them into a single system with more than 1,000 qubits. The current Nighthawk r2, introduced in September, delivers up to 100,000 samples per second and handles more than 7,500 operations on 120 qubits. Partners including E.ON, Algorithmiq, and Q-CTRL have run 34 billion circuits on it and report improvements of up to 19x.

IBM also published a report called Proof, Not Promises: The Digital Sovereignty Index. It finds an overconfidence gap, where leaders think they can prove sovereign control of their technology, but the evidence often falls short. 94 percent of enterprises surveyed faced at least one sovereignty challenge in the past three years, and among those, 81 percent had to give formal evidence to regulators, customers, auditors, or internal stakeholders. For your AI adoption conversations, the takeaway is that control now has to be demonstrable.

And at Web Summit Lisbon, IBM named LivXP the winner of its Sports Tech Startup Prize Challenge. LivXP is a Swiss-American company out of the Harvard Innovation Labs, and it uses agentic AI for fan engagement and commerce, like auctions and sponsor activations, in 150 countries. Seven finalists pitched, and the judges included Ferrari ambassador Marc Gené and CNN's Brad Smith.
