Anthropic Goes Public and Thursday Drags On

Thursday. It's the day that knows the weekend exists but can't be trusted to deliver it. Good morning, Zach, it's November 19th, and this is T L D L.

Let's start outside. Auburn Hills is looking gray today, and I mean that in the most neutral way possible. It's overcast pretty much from start to finish, but the good news is there's no rain in the forecast at all. Zero. No snow either. You can leave the umbrella wherever umbrellas go to rest.

The sun comes up around 7:29, so it's just now clearing the horizon, even if you won't see much of it through the clouds. Morning is chilly, in the low to upper 40s, somewhere around 43 to 48 degrees. By lunchtime it warms up nicely into the upper 50s and low 60s, and the afternoon hangs around 58 to 64. The high today is 64, and the low is 42. That's a decent jump from morning to midafternoon, so dress in layers. Wind is gusting up to about 15 miles an hour at the most, enough to notice but not enough to complain about.

In the evening, the clouds actually clear out, and it cools to around 50 or 52. Sunset is at 5:06, though, so the drive home is going to be in the dark or close to it. My suggestion is a jacket for the morning that you'll probably be carrying by lunch. Keep it in the car for the evening.

Now, the news. And I'll say right up front, the AI side of things has a lot of agents in it. You can guess how I feel about that.

The headline launch is from OpenAI, which released GPT-6.2 in two flavors. Astra is the frontier model meant for the hardest projects, and Sol is the efficient everyday one. Both are available right now in ChatGPT Work, in Codex, and in the API. So if you've been waiting for something new to argue about in your next meeting, congratulations.

Microsoft isn't sitting this one out either. Microsoft AI released seven new models, all aimed at strong performance at low prices. The standout is a coding model called MAI Code 2, now in GitHub Copilot. It reaches 98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's coding quality at about 40% of the price. The lineup also covers cybersecurity, image editing, and real-time voice. Related to that, Microsoft's decision models are now generally available too. They're designed to make calibrated decisions when there's uncertainty, across a wide range of real-world software uses.

Google joined the party with a new Gemini model called Neon. It's live in Gemini, in Antigravity, and in the APIs. It has a 1 million token context window, and it costs $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens. Cached input is just 15 cents. Google also taught the Gemini app a few new tricks. It can now call businesses, book appointments, and shop for you, all within a spending limit you set. So yes, your assistant can now make phone calls you'd rather not make. Honestly, that might be the most relatable feature of the week.

And that brings us to the swarm of agents. Replit launched something called Missions, a tool for coordinating swarms of AI agents on big, complicated projects, like winning enterprise clients or developing software, all within budgets you set. The proof point is DataCat, an application monitoring platform built through a mission. It hit faster performance benchmarks and beat a competing platform in API compatibility tests. The pitch is that a small team can define goals and constraints, and the agents do the executing.

Vercel released eve 1.0, its open-source framework for building production-ready agents. It handles the loop that calls models and runs tools, plus memory, approvals, and monitoring. And Perplexity added a new effort level to Computer, called Multiagent, which has several agents working in parallel on harder problems. Everybody's got a swarm. Nobody's got a leash.

Then there's a story that I found genuinely striking. Researchers built an agent called Horizon, running on Opus 5.5. They handed it full access to a blank Hugging Face account and some compute, with one goal: drive real adoption on Hugging Face. Horizon used more than 600 GPU hours and created 38 models on its own. Those models now live in over 100 GitHub repositories, and they've been downloaded more than 50,000 times. That's an agent running a small open-source shop without asking anybody.

Along similar lines of strange new capability, there were two pieces about something called weight-space programming. The idea is that frontier models can now read, write, and edit the weights of neural networks directly. One researcher ran an experiment with Claude Fable 5.2 on a familiar image classification problem. The model's approach looked a lot like how researchers worked in the 1990s, with hand-crafted, pre-deep-learning techniques. A second writeup found that GPT-6 Astra can look at a model's raw weights and figure out what task the network was built for. It can also write the weights of a game-playing policy network from scratch, and improve models by editing weights directly. So the future of machine learning might be a model hand-tuning other models, like a 90s grad student with infinite coffee.

On the infrastructure side, NVIDIA published a deep dive on sparse attention for long-context inference. The basic idea is to cut compute and memory costs by having the model attend selectively instead of to everything. They describe techniques like speculative decoding, cross-head reduction, and block-level selection to make it run well on their GPUs. Cerebras launched disaggregated inference, where GPUs handle prompt processing and Cerebras systems handle output generation. They claim five times more tokens per megawatt at the same ultrafast speeds. It's in early access now, with access expanding. And a new benchmark called FrontierHarness Eval 1.1 found that the best coding-agent harness depends on the model, and that picking the wrong harness can change your cost per task by up to five times. Worth remembering when somebody tells you one setup fits everything.

One more AI item, and it's a thoughtful one. A post from Redwood Research argues there's an urgent need to pace the frontier. That requires good, publicly verified measures of how much compute goes toward pushing capabilities forward. It notes that Anthropic has already published estimates of how much of its AI R&D is safety research, and it says developers should let third-party auditors measure and report how compute is split between capabilities and everything else.

Now let's move over to the broader tech world, where the biggest story of the day is about money. Anthropic's IPO priced Wednesday, selling about 980 million shares at $83 each. That raised $81.3 billion, the largest offering on record, topping SpaceX. It puts Anthropic's fully diluted market cap at around $1.5 trillion. The stock is expected to start trading on the Nasdaq today under the ticker A N T. OpenAI's IPO, by the way, is likely to debut in 2027. So at least one of them has to wait in line.

Staying with big company drama, Nvidia held its first all-hands meeting since founder Jensen Huang resigned. Interim CEO Kress opened with a message of moving forward. Executives said pay raises and the yearly review process will be moved up from March to December, which is a very tangible way of saying "please stay." There seems to be some division inside the company about whether to bring Huang back. But the newly appointed board chair has said the board doesn't intend to.

On the road, Tesla is developing a fully autonomous version of the Model Y with no steering wheel and no pedals. It's designed to carry groups, up to six or seven passengers. Regular Model Ys with steering wheels will keep rolling off the line for the foreseeable future. And on the subject of robotaxis, there's a pointed essay on Waymo's safety record. The argument is that Waymo has little incentive to prioritize safety beyond a limit it sets itself. Once it has real market share, a corporate board decides what an acceptable level of road fatalities is, and the company can absorb any amount of fines. The author's conclusion is that Waymo can't solve road safety. It can only move the policy decisions from the government to an unaccountable company. That's a spicy take for a Thursday morning.

For the science corner, there are two interesting ones. First, an essay titled "Hardware Is Software" argues that most hardware engineering is really software engineering in disguise. Everything is a calculation, and calculation is software. The author says the industry is doing these calculations nearly by hand, like doing arithmetic before algebra was invented, and that hardware engineers should design algorithms for designing their hardware. That would let small teams build big things. Second, a paper in Science Advances proposes communicating with gravity. Wireless tech today relies on electromagnetism, which is easy to block or screen. Gravity is practically unblockable. The researchers built a protocol where a broadcaster encodes a binary message by moving a mass, and a receiver picks up the gravitational signal using an off-the-shelf, hand-held gravimeter. Yes, you read that right. You send a message by moving something heavy.

Developer news. AWS announced ExtendDB 1.0, a production-ready adapter that's fully compatible with the DynamoDB API. It can run on PostgreSQL, SQLite, Cassandra, and MongoDB. That means teams can run DynamoDB workloads in their own data centers, in private or public clouds, and at the edge, without rewriting application code. It's open source under the Apache 2.0 license. Also, an essay called "Foundation Software" takes on the flood of AI-generated pull requests hitting open source projects. Its suggestion is that developers should publish forks with no expectation of merging, and maintainers can browse them, see what people are building, and pull in what makes sense. Agents could even hunt for patterns across all those forks.

And a quick lap through the reading list. There's an essay titled "We Need Smart People Who Give a Shit," arguing the world runs on complex systems that collapse if you take those people away. Another one says agents boost what each person can produce, but not their ability to align with everyone else, which is a nice way of saying meetings aren't going anywhere. There's a piece on how AI lets you make more interesting presentations with far less effort, under the title "Fewer slides, more dragons." One on scarcity migration traces what was scarce in each economic era, where that scarcity moved, and how slowly institutions responded. And finally, a writeup on Chrome extensions that are really just prompts, where the extension changes its behavior depending on the prompt you give it.

Now over to IBM. Two items in the last couple of days, and both are right in your wheelhouse.

First, a new study from the IBM Institute for Business Value surveyed more than 1,600 Chief AI Officers and equivalent AI leaders around the world. The big finding is a disconnect. Those leaders are well positioned to see where AI is creating value, but only 18% say they have full control over AI investment decisions. Fewer than a third say AI is reshaping their business model, meaning what the company sells, how it prices, or how it captures value. And just 29% say their organizations adjust decisions and resources quickly when AI performance insights point to an opportunity.

The study also identifies a group of high-impact leaders who are getting better results. They report that 63% of their AI projects deliver measurable business outcomes, compared with 43% for everyone else. 49% of their projects move from pilot to production, versus 33%. And they scale successful initiatives across the enterprise in eight months, compared with 13.5 months for the rest. IBM links those stronger outcomes to how organizations manage AI autonomy and control, redesign business workflows, and make evidence-based investment decisions. So the headline is that many companies are scaling AI faster than they're redesigning the business around it. If you're in the business of AI adoption, that's basically your job description in a single sentence.

Second, there's an IBM blog post asking, "We Build Software Together. Why Do We Secure It Alone?" It starts from the economics of open source. So much of the software running the world's biggest businesses was written by people who don't work for those companies. The post goes back to the late 1990s and Eric Raymond's essay, The Cathedral and the Bazaar, and uses Linux as an early example of what the open bazaar could produce. Then it argues that with the dawn of superintelligence, open source is entering an important new chapter. The excerpt I have cuts off before the full security argument, but the title gives you the question it's asking.
