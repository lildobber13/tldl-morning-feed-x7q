Staying on chips, the US Commerce Department is closing a loophole. Foreign rivals had been getting around export restrictions by renting access to advanced chips in data centers in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil. Now companies headquartered in countries seen as higher risk for diversion to China, Russia, and Iran will need a license, with strict know-your-customer controls, when they move high volumes of advanced chips. Licensed companies will file monthly reports detailing everyone involved in each transaction. The rule takes effect 90 days after it's posted to the Federal Register. So the paperwork gets thicker, which is how you know it's serious.

On the Chinese side, top AI developers there are holding back their models' ability to help with online attacks. That comes after a government proposal to ban exporting that kind of technology. Self-censorship by anticipation. Very efficient.

On to the model releases. Alibaba's Qwen4-Max is pitched as a new teammate. It's said to handle creative and professional work across 3D modeling, game development, precision manufacturing, finance, law, healthcare, and frontier science. It can drive Blender to model geometry, assemble scenes, set up materials and lighting, and render. It works with the leading game engines and can deliver a playable game, with scene construction, gameplay logic, HUD design, and visual effects. Smaller models in the family are coming to open source. That's a big résumé for a teammate who doesn't need a lunch break.

At the other end of the scale, there's Embla 1, a reasoning model built for phones. It's meant to be fast and use few tokens, and it runs privately, offline, and for free. At 4 bits it averages 57.4 across a set of benchmarks, and it answers in 1.77 seconds end to end on an iPhone 18 Pro. Small, quick, and it doesn't phone home.

Small is also the theme of Quark, a language model with about 165 million parameters. It was trained for roughly $4,200 of compute, about 1,300 H-100 hours. It posts the best benchmark average among open models of its size trained on similar token budgets. The team built it to test every part of their training stack before spending serious money on a big model. Practice run, with receipts.

Then there's a deep dive from Thinking Machines on training language models in 4-bit precision. It looks at the quantization errors and how they hurt accuracy, and compares formats called BF16, MXFP8, and NVFP4. Fewer bits, more problems, but also more savings, so people keep trying.

Now the thinking pieces. One essay called On the Brutality of Amdahl's Law makes a sobering point. AI is predicted to bring huge economic growth, but the world is too slow to absorb it. Electricity, communication, computers, transportation, and agriculture all went through extreme transformations, yet we've only seen about 2% GDP per capita growth for nearly 150 years. In other words, the bottleneck isn't the genius, it's everything else. As someone who works on getting AI adopted, you may feel seen.

Now to IBM, where there's a good trio of stories today.

First, an IBM Institute for Business Value study of more than 1,600 Chief AI Officers and equivalent leaders. The headline is that these folks can see where AI creates value, but few control the decisions to act on it. Only 18% say they have full control over AI investment decisions. Fewer than a third say AI is reshaping their business model, meaning what the company sells, how it prices, or how it captures value. Just 29% say their organizations adjust decisions and resources quickly when AI performance insights show an opportunity. The study also identifies a group of high-impact CAIOs with better results. They report that 63% of their AI projects deliver measurable business outcomes, against 43% for everyone else. 49% move from pilot to production, versus 33%. And they scale successful work enterprise-wide in eight months, compared with 13.5 months for the rest. The study ties that to how organizations manage AI autonomy and control, redesign workflows, and make evidence-based investment calls. If you work in AI adoption, that's a very quotable set of numbers.

Second, IBM announced plans with NVIDIA and Fermilab for a new co-design platform for modular quantum computers. It would put NVIDIA DGX B200 systems alongside IBM quantum computers, including ones powered by IBM Quantum Nighthawk. That's IBM's most powerful quantum processor yet, running circuits of up to 7,500 two-qubit operations on 120 qubits, at up to 100,000 samples per second. Fermilab's Superconducting Quantum Materials and Systems Center is part of the work. The goal is to see how multiple quantum processors can work together and how classical compute can scale alongside them. Fermilab and IBM will also look at calculations in high-energy and nuclear physics that classical systems can't handle alone, like lattice gauge theories, neutrino interactions, and nuclear simulations.

Third, IBM and AMD released Pelagos, an open-source, GPU-accelerated framework for evaluating quantum error-correction decoders at scale. Proving a decoder works can mean evaluating billions of sampled error patterns, which takes huge simulation campaigns. Pelagos is meant to make that faster, more scalable, and more accessible. It follows the earlier announcement with Oak Ridge National Laboratory and AMD, and it's coming out at SC26 along with new research on scaling across multiple GPUs, plus a survey paper on decoding and hardware-software co-design. Quantum's big problem is errors, and apparently the fix is a lot of very fast classical computers.
