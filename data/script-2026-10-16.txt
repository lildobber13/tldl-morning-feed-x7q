Now to IBM.

First, IBM published the inaugural edition of Open Source Security Signals for the third quarter. The central idea is that counting vulnerabilities isn't enough. A critical flaw may pose little real exposure in your environment, while a lower severity one can get much worse when paired with another weakness or with compromised credentials. Finding the flaw is only the start. Teams have to understand the risk, decide on action, find a fix, and get it to the software that needs it. That's a useful framing for any client conversation about open source risk.

Second, a new IBM study of more than 3,200 senior executives across eight countries in Europe and the Middle East. Eight in ten say an AI related incident, like an agent failure, data exposure, or decision error, reached their board or C suite in the past year. But only 40 percent changed their AI governance policy in response. A majority, 56 percent, press ahead within agreed risk limits, and only about a third delay deployment until governance is ready. Thirty five percent are running agentic AI in production already, and three quarters expect agents to have some autonomy in decisions by 2027. Yet only 37 percent say their governance is designed to scale with agentic AI. IBM's message is that governance has to be built into how agents operate, with clear boundaries, clear ownership, and real time controls. That's a squarely on topic set of numbers for your day job.

Third, IBM and Siemens are collaborating on research to advance IBM's nanostack architecture. Nanostack is a three dimensional, nanosheet based transistor design that vertically stacks and staggers transistors, packing nearly 100 billion of them into a space the size of a fingernail at the current 7 angstrom node. IBM demonstrated it earlier this year, and now the work is getting it ready for manufacturing at scale. Siemens brings its design automation, simulation, and lithography expertise, including virtual models to find problems before chips are built.
